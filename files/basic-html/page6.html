                                            T I M E










         He learned that lesson when he closed The Actors Network. After investing 23½ years in something
         that had become closely connected to his identity, West prepared carefully for its ending, handed
         over  the  keys  and  walked  away.  He  did  not  spend  the  next  decade  trying  to  recreate  what  had
         been. The organization became part of his history.

         He  anticipates  approaching  other  transitions  similarly.  His  direct  union  service  and  some  of  his
         actor-business education work are finite chapters. He has invested time in developing others who
         can continue the work, and that makes stepping away easier. Eventually, as West puts it, you “pass
         the baton.” Someone else steps forward and becomes the leader.


         There  is  a  maturity  in  that  idea  that  extends  far  beyond  West's  career.  We  spend  enormous
         amounts of energy learning how to begin things and relatively little learning how to end them. Yet a
         meaningful  life  inevitably  contains  both.  Careers  change,  organizations  evolve,  children  grow,
         identities shift and roles that once defined us eventually belong to someone else. West increasingly
         sees a successful ending not as evidence that something has been lost, but as evidence that it has
         been completed.


         None  of  this  means  his  ambitions  have  disappeared.  His  vision  for  the  next  five  years  includes
         speaking regularly to corporate audiences around the world about communication and leadership,
         producing independent films and continuing to travel. He hopes to produce two independent films
         a year while creating enough freedom to experience more of the world personally.


         Beyond that, however, his ambitions begin to change. He imagines fewer but longer trips, perhaps
         one  major  artistic  project  a  year,  less  speaking  and  more  peace  and  quiet.  There  may  be  more
         books  to  write,  but  the  commodity  he  increasingly  values  is  not  another  credit  or  another
         accomplishment. It is focused time.

         West makes a fascinating observation about money. It cannot buy happiness, he says, but it can
         buy focused time. Despite everything he has accomplished, focused time is something he believes
         he  has  not  enjoyed  enough.  He  has  spent  much  of  his  life  being  busy,  and  being  busy  is  not
         necessarily the same thing as being present.
